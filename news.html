    Cyprus News

    Politics, society, education, the environment and the island's place in Europe, explained without the jargon.

    Ad placeholder · 728×90 / 320×100

    Public life in Cyprus is compact enough that a single decision in Nicosia can reach every household on the island within a news cycle, yet layered enough that understanding any one story usually means understanding three others first. A debate about school hours turns out to hinge on the summer heat and the structure of the working day. A row about coastal development leads back through planning law, tourism policy and the slow legal history of title deeds. And behind many stories sits the division of the island itself, a fact of geography and politics that has shaped every institution here for half a century.

    That is why this section leans on explainers rather than raw headlines. The daily record of announcements, votes and appointments is well covered elsewhere, in Greek and in English. What is harder to find is the connective tissue: how the House of Representatives actually passes a law, why municipal reform mattered enough to redraw the map of local government, what the education system asks of families, and how the environment is holding up under a construction boom and a warming Mediterranean climate. Each guide below is written to stand on its own, so you can start with whichever question brought you here. Together they form a working manual for following Cypriot public life in English, whether you were born on the island, arrived last month, or follow it from abroad. Where a topic deserves book-length treatment, we say so, and point to where the deeper reading lives.

    How government works: a presidential republic inside the EU

    The Republic of Cyprus is the European Union's only presidential republic. There is no prime minister. The president, elected directly for a five-year term, is both head of state and head of government, appoints the Council of Ministers, and does not sit in or answer to the legislature. The arrangement dates to the 1960 constitution, drafted for a two-community state, and it survived the upheavals that followed because so much of the constitution became difficult to amend without touching questions the island has never resolved.

    Law-making runs through the House of Representatives, a single-chamber parliament in Nicosia. Bills usually begin life in a ministry, pass through the law office for legal vetting, and arrive at the House where committees do most of the substantive work: hearing civil servants, professional bodies and unions before a text ever reaches the floor. Because the executive does not command a parliamentary majority by design, coalitions form issue by issue. A government bill can be amended beyond recognition or parked in committee for years, and members of the House can introduce their own proposals, though anything with a budgetary cost needs government consent to become law.

    The president may refer a passed law back to the House, or to the Supreme Constitutional Court if the objection is constitutional rather than political. Referrals are not rare, and the court's rulings have shaped everything from pension law to the boundaries of parliamentary power. Once signed, laws take effect on publication in the official gazette.

    Where the EU fits in

    Membership since 2004 added a second legal ecosystem on top of this machinery. Regulations from Brussels apply directly; directives must be transposed by the House, and a noticeable share of its calendar is exactly that. The euro, adopted in 2008, moved monetary policy to Frankfurt, while the Central Bank of Cyprus remains the national supervisor within the eurozone's banking framework. One consequence matters more here than anywhere else in the Union: EU law formally covers the whole island, but its application is suspended in the north pending a settlement, a legal formula that keeps the door open without pretending the division does not exist.

    Local government, redrawn

    Below the national level, a long-debated reform consolidated dozens of municipalities and communities into a smaller set of larger local authorities, paired with district organisations that handle water, sewerage and building permits at scale. For residents, the practical effect is that the office issuing your building permit or collecting your local fees may now sit one town over, with decisions intended to be more uniform across each district: Nicosia, Limassol, Larnaca, Paphos and the government-controlled part of Famagusta.

    Education: two systems side by side

    Ask a parent in Cyprus about schools and you will usually hear about a choice, because the island effectively runs two systems in parallel. The public system, free and taught in Greek, takes children from pre-primary through lyceum, with a technical and vocational track that has been steadily modernised. The school day starts early and ends early by northern European standards, a rhythm built around the climate, and the afternoon institution known as the frontistirio, private tutoring in everything from English to physics, fills the gap for a large share of families preparing for university entrance exams.

    Alongside it runs one of the largest private, English-language school sectors in Europe relative to population. These schools grew up serving expatriate families and Cypriots planning on British universities, and many follow a curriculum leading to IGCSEs and A-levels or the International Baccalaureate. Fees vary widely between cities and schools, so visiting and comparing remains essential; the schools themselves publish current fee schedules, and no guide stays accurate for long.

    The university story

    Higher education is the quiet success story of the modern Cypriot economy. A generation ago most school-leavers went abroad to study. Today the University of Cyprus, the Cyprus University of Technology in Limassol and a cluster of private universities in Nicosia, Limassol and Paphos teach tens of thousands of students, a substantial share of them international. English-language degrees in medicine, business and computing draw students from dozens of countries, and the sector now ranks among the island's more dependable earners, filling apartments, cafés and part-time jobs in every university neighbourhood.

    For prospective students the practical points are these: public universities teach mainly in Greek at undergraduate level with growing English-language postgraduate provision; private universities teach mainly in English; recognition of degrees across the EU follows the standard European framework; and student residence permits for non-EU nationals run through the migration department with the university usually guiding the paperwork. Anyone comparing offers should check accreditation status directly with the state's quality assurance agency, which publishes its register online.

    Environment: a dry island under a heavy load

    Cyprus has no permanent rivers and gets almost all of its rain in a short winter window, which makes water the island's oldest policy problem. The response, built over decades, is a national network of dams linked by pipelines and canals, topped up by desalination plants along the coast that can carry the cities through a failed winter. Households feel the system's logic in their bills and occasionally in restrictions on garden use during drought years, and farmers feel it in the allocation decisions that decide which crops get irrigated water in a dry summer.

    Climate pressure is not hypothetical here. Summers have grown hotter and longer, and the fire season with them. The Troodos forests, largely state-managed and loved with real intensity by Cypriots, are patrolled through the dry months by a dedicated forest service with aircraft on standby; village-level volunteer groups and strict rules on open flames do much of the quiet preventive work. After a serious fire, replanting programmes run for years, and the burnt hillsides visible from motorways serve as a public reminder of what a single spark costs.

    The coastline question

    The third pressure is development itself. Tourism and construction are pillars of the economy, and both concentrate on the same narrow coastal strip that hosts the island's most sensitive habitats, including beaches where green and loggerhead turtles nest. The Akamas peninsula in the west has become the emblem of the argument: how much access, how much building, and how much protection a place can bear at once. Environmental impact assessment is required for significant projects, EU habitat rules apply through the Natura 2000 network, and court challenges by environmental groups have become a regular feature of planning life. The pattern repeats at smaller scale in every district: a proposed marina, a hillside licence, a road through maquis scrubland, each testing where the balance sits between an economy that builds and a landscape that cannot be rebuilt.

    The Green Line: living with Europe's last divided capital

    Cross Nicosia's old town from south to north and you will meet a line of oil drums, sandbags and shuttered houses where the city simply stops. The buffer zone, patrolled by the United Nations force UNFICYP since 1964 and fixed in its present course by the events of 1974, runs roughly 180 kilometres across the island, from Kato Pyrgos in the west to the fenced-off resort district of Varosha in the east. In places it is wide farmland; in the heart of the capital it narrows to the width of a single street.

    Since 2003, crossing points have allowed movement in both directions, and the crossings have become part of ordinary life: workers commuting, families visiting, shoppers comparing prices, tourists walking from one side of Ledra Street to the other in an afternoon. EU citizens cross with an identity card or passport; the checks are quick but real. Trade across the line is governed by the EU's Green Line Regulation, which allows certain goods produced in the north to move south under supervision, one of many pieces of legal machinery invented for a situation with no exact precedent anywhere.

    The vocabulary of coverage can be confusing, so a plain-language key helps. The Republic of Cyprus is the internationally recognised state and the EU member. The area north of the line is administered by an entity recognised only by Turkey. The United Nations runs the buffer zone between them and hosts the on-and-off negotiations aimed at reunifying the island as a federation, talks that have come close more than once, most recently before collapsing at Crans-Montana in 2017. Bi-communal projects, from a shared sewage system for Nicosia to joint teams searching for persons missing since the conflict, carry on regardless of the diplomatic weather, quiet proof that the two halves of the city never fully stopped cooperating.

    Key things to know

    • Cyprus is a presidential republic: the president heads both state and government, and the House of Representatives legislates.
    • The island joined the EU in 2004 and the eurozone in 2008; EU law is suspended in the areas outside government control.
    • The Green Line has crossing points open since 2003; EU citizens cross with an ID card or passport.
    • Public schooling is in Greek and free; a large private sector teaches in English.
    • Water and wildfire are the island's defining environmental pressures, managed through dams, desalination and a dedicated forest service.
